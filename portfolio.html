The Un-Rebalanced 30

A 30-name equal-weight portfolio meant to be bought once and left alone. With twenty years of tape instead of two, the screen can finally be tested properly — and the answer is that it only works if you actually hold it.

Tape 2006-07-03 → 2026-08-07 Trading days 5,056 Bars 15.2M Investable 1,913 Passed screen 262 Held 30
Answer

30 names, 3.33% each, nine sectors, no trades. Wired into the tracker as the benchmark FYP30.

Finding

The edge is entirely a function of holding period. Re-formed every year it loses to an equal-weight basket by 3.0 points a year. Held three years or more it wins — +2.5 points at 3 years, +2.4 at 7, beating equal weight in 9 of 13 and 7 of 9 vintages. This is a holding rule, not a trading signal.

Correction

My earlier report called missing dividends "the largest single distortion". That was wrong. The close column is already a total-return series; only the adj_close field is NULL, and I read the field name instead of testing the data. Every return below includes reinvested dividends.

Caveat

Against a cap-weighted universe the edge is a coin flip (15.4% vs 14.7% across start years). The reliable win is over equal weight. And the long-horizon vintages overlap heavily, so treat the t-statistics as descriptive.

At one year the screen is actively harmful: it loses to a random basket 60% of the time. The sign flips at two years and the advantage is established by three. That is what you would expect from a rule that selects durable, cash-generative businesses — the quality shows up in compounding, not in the next four earnings prints, and over twelve months the defensive tilt just costs you beta in risk-on years.

Read the overlap warning seriously. A 10-year column built from annual start dates on a 20-year tape has at most two non-overlapping windows. The six vintages there share most of their history with each other. The 1- and 2-year rows are close to independent; the 7- and 10-year rows are close to a single observation dressed up as several.

Why thirty names

Re-derived at the horizon that actually applies. Dispersion between single stocks grows with the square root of time, so a basket sized for one year is undersized for ten.

Sector mix. Six sectors hold 4 names each (13.3%) — Financials, Technology, Energy, Materials, Consumer Cyclical, Healthcare — with Industrials 3, Communication Services 2 and Consumer Defensive 1. A hard cap of four per sector binds on all six; without it Energy and Financials would dominate.

The screen. Positive trailing earnings, operating income and free cash flow; operating margin above 5%, gross margin above 20%; revenue growing; margins not collapsing; ROE between 10% and 200%; equity at least 15% of assets; net debt under 3× EBITDA; earnings yield above 2%; at least $2bn market cap and $20m a day of volume; no drawdown worse than 60% in the prior year. 262 of 1,913 names clear it. Ranking inside that pool uses four equally-weighted pillars — quality, growth, value and cash conversion — with no fitted coefficients.

The portfolio is now a benchmark in ~/services/finance, not just a table in a report.

A benchmark there used to have to be a ticker. It can now be a basket: a JSON file in composites/ names the holdings and weights, the sync path fetches the constituents instead of the basket, and the basket is rebuilt from them. Everything downstream — growth chart, scorecard, beta, alpha — treats it as an ordinary symbol. Baskets are held un-rebalanced, which is the point rather than a simplification.

Defects in this database

Each one changed a number. The first is a correction to my own earlier report.

  1. I was wrong that returns excluded dividends. adj_close is NULL for all 15.2M rows, and I concluded the closes were price-only. They are not: close is already split- and dividend-adjusted. AT&T's June 2009 close is $5.53 while it paid $0.41 a quarter — a 28% yield if the series were raw. Its only post-2009 split explains 1.3× of the 4.3× gap; the residual is reinvested dividends. Realty Income's 20-year "price" CAGR here is 10.9%, which is its real-world total return. Applying dividends on top manufactured a 30%/yr yield for NVDA before I caught it.
  2. The dividends endpoint silently truncates at 50 rows. /dividends/{sym} takes an undocumented limit; without it you get ~26 records after de-duplication, so roughly six years. With limit=5000 Coca-Cola returns 370 records back to 1970. The first pull took the default and would have under-adjusted everything before 2020.
  3. amount and adj_amount differ by up to 200×. amount is cash as paid; adj_amount restates it into split-adjusted share terms. NVDA's 2012 dividend is $0.075 as paid and $0.0019 adjusted. Using the wrong one against a split-adjusted close is a 40× error per payment.
  4. The income statement was capped at 12 quarters while the balance sheet got 80. Both come from /fundamentals/{sym}/history, but the original pull passed limit=12. Income was therefore the binding constraint on how far back any point-in-time test could run: every formation date before 2024 had an empty universe and the whole backtest silently collapsed to a single year. Re-pulling at limit=80 took the table from 72,525 rows to 270,963 and the mean symbol from 12 quarters to 43.6.
  5. ETF prices are unusable and ETF dividends do not exist. SPY has 565 bars across 19 years — 30 a year against 251 for any common stock — and /dividends/SPY returns [], as does QQQ, VTI, RSP, DIA and TLT. There is no index benchmark to be had here, which is why every benchmark in this report is built from individual stocks.
  6. A market-cap filter deleted every delisted name. Carried over from the first report and still true: symbols.market_cap is a pull-time snapshot, so deriving shares against the panel's last date yields NULL for anything that stopped trading, and a notna() filter then removes exactly the companies that died. Shares are now backed out at each symbol's own last traded price.

What I would tell you before you buy it

  • Do not judge it for three years. That is not an excuse written in advance — it is the measured result. At a one-year horizon this screen is worse than random, and the whole case for it rests on horizons you have to actually sit through.
  • Cap-weighting is nearly as good and far less work. Across start years the screen made 15.4% against 14.7% for simply cap-weighting the same investable universe. If you would not enjoy owning 30 individual positions, that gap is not worth it.
  • Survivorship still flatters these numbers slightly. Over a 15-year hold, 6.5% of the universe stops printing. Their last price is a median 26% below their own peak and 8 of 50 ended more than 80% down, so they are mostly acquisitions rather than wipeouts — but a company that fails quietly leaves the tape rather than printing its way to zero, and that biases every long-hold return upward.
  • Half the portfolio has a short history. Only 17 of 30 names have a full 20-year tape; RDDT, ARIS, BLBD and DAVE are recent listings with no record through a real bear market. They score well on current fundamentals and are the least proven.
  • The rule would name different companies next year. It is a point-in-time snapshot of cheap-and-profitable, not a list of permanent compounders. Holding it untouched is the strategy that was tested — but do not imagine the same 30 names would screen well in 2031.
