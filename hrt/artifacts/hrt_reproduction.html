Reproducing the Hierarchical Reinforced Trader

A bi-level PPO–DDPG trader reports beating the S&P 500 in both a bull and a bear year. Rebuilt from the paper text on point-in-time index data, it lands well short of the published figures when its signal is timed honestly, and well past them when the signal is timed as the paper specifies. The published result sits in between — in an interval that requires the look-ahead to be there.

01

What reproduced

Headline claim

Under causal timing HRT-FR returns -6.98% in 2022 against a published +2.29%, and +21.05% in 2021 against +39.83%. Neither year reproduces.

But the leak overshoots

Wired to the timing the paper specifies, the same agent returns +100.1% and +67.8% — far above the published figures, not equal to them. The published numbers sit between the two arms.

Passive floor

Deploying the cash once and sitting still, inside the same environment, returns +26.47% and -5.64%. Most agents here do not clear it in the bear year.

Motivation

The pathology the paper is built to fix is real. Standalone DDPG collapses to buy-and-hold — its whole annual turnover is the day-one deployment — while the hierarchy trades throughout.

02

The signal leaks before any agent is trained

The high-level controller is fed a predicted forward return, defined in the paper as the change in opening price from day T to day T+1, produced from Qlib Alpha158 features observed through day T. Day T’s close sits inside that window. Alpha158 carries KMID = (close−open)/open and twelve more features built on the same bar, so the first leg of the target is handed to the model as an input.

Qlib’s own Alpha158 handler labels with Ref($close,-2)/Ref($close,-1)-1, which skips precisely this window. The paper departs from that default without remarking on it.

What this means

A 1,089× return in a single year is not a trading result; it is a model reading tomorrow’s bar. No implementation was released, so it cannot be confirmed that the published numbers were produced this way. What can be said is that the procedure as described is not implementable without leakage, and that every figure below uses the causal reading instead.

03

PPO does not survive a 3N action space unchanged

The high-level controller picks one of three directions for each of 370 stocks, a factorised categorical over 3370 joint actions. A stock PPO sums the entropy bonus and the importance ratio across action dimensions, which is harmless at the handful of dimensions such code is normally run on. At N = 370 the entropy bonus is 0.01 × 370 × ln 3 ≈ 4.06 against a policy-gradient term of order 0.01. The optimiser simply maximises entropy and the policy never leaves uniform random.

This is easy to miss, because a random high-level policy still produces plausible-looking returns — it deploys capital and tracks the market. It only shows up if you ask the controller to learn something it cannot fail at.

Two corrections restore learning, and both follow the paper rather than departing from it. The clipped objective and entropy are taken per dimension and averaged. And credit is assigned per stock: the paper’s HLC reward is α·Σi rialign + (1−α)·rl, a sum in which each term depends only on that stock’s own action — collapsing it to one scalar before the update throws away exactly the structure that makes 370 simultaneous decisions learnable.

Why the α schedule now matters

The paper sets αt = e−0.001t without saying what t counts. Per env step, α is spent by step 3,000 of 500,000 — per-stock credit is switched off for 99.4% of training and the controller is left learning 370 decisions from a single scalar. Per episode, it decays across the whole run. Both readings are reported below; the difference is not cosmetic.

Read leak-free, HRT-FR reaches +21.1% in 2021 and -7.0% in 2022, against published figures of +39.8% and +2.3%. The paper’s ordering does not survive either: it reports HRT-FR > DDPG > PPO in both years, where here the two flat agents finish ahead of the hierarchy in 2021 (+29.1% and +27.3% against +21.1%) and the three sit within a point and a half of each other in 2022. Every one of them, the hierarchy included, finishes below a portfolio that bought once and then did nothing.

Wired to the timing the paper specifies, the same code overshoots the published numbers by a wide margin — and does so with unusually tight seed agreement, because an agent reading tomorrow’s bar has little left to be uncertain about. So the published figures are not what full exploitation of the stated timing produces either.

What lies between the two ends is partial exploitation. The defective agent of section 03 is one way to arrive there: on the leaked signal but with crippled credit assignment it returned +21.3% and −9.7%, below the published values, while the corrected agent on the same signal returns +78% and +54%, above them. A weaker forecaster, a partially overlapping feature window, or a less efficient learner would each land somewhere inside that interval. None of this identifies what the authors ran — no code was released — but the interval that reproduces their numbers is one that requires the look-ahead to be present.

Two of the three pathologies the paper sets out to fix do measurably improve. Standalone PPO and DDPG barely trade at all — a daily turnover of 0.4% is entirely the day-one deployment of cash, and PPO touches 1.4 names a day and only 165 of 370 names all year. The hierarchy trades 21–27 names a day, reaches essentially the whole universe, and halves the concentration of what it trades. Inertia and narrow diversification are real, and the bi-level structure genuinely addresses both.

But look at the last two columns. Gross of transaction costs, HRT-FR on an honest signal returns -1.34% in 2022 against a passive floor of -5.64% — 4.3 points of genuine edge. It then pays 5.64 points in commission to collect it, and finishes below the floor. At 24% daily turnover the strategy churns its book roughly 62 times a year, and a flat 10 bp cost assumption is doing an enormous amount of load-bearing work.

Where this points

The interesting quantity in this architecture is not whether the hierarchy beats a flat agent — it does, gross — but whether it can be made to pay for itself. That makes turnover penalties and a realistic, non-constant cost model the load-bearing part of any follow-on work, not a detail to be bolted on afterwards. Notably, the paper's own v2 revision moves in exactly this direction, adding an explicit turnover budget and a turnover penalty to the low-level reward.

08

The bear-year result is mostly the universe

The paper’s most striking claim is 2022: HRT holds a small positive return while the S&P 500 loses 20%. But the benchmark and the trading universe are not the same population.

Of the 503 constituents reconstructed as of January 2015, 133 have no usable price history in the data available here — and 108 of those 133 are names the index itself removed during 2015–2022. What survives is the cohort that was never acquired and never failed. Holding all 370 of them in equal weight, with no model whatsoever, returns −5.40% in 2022 against the index’s −19.91%. Fourteen points of apparent skill are available before anything is learned.

This is not a defect peculiar to this reproduction. yfinance, the source the paper names, cannot retrieve most of those delisted tickers at all, so any “S&P 500” universe assembled from it is filtered the same way — probably more severely, since the reconstruction here at least keeps 44 names the index dropped.

The gap is not an artefact of the dividend convention either. Reconstructing a price-only panel from the dividend record puts the same equal-weight basket at −7.66% for 2022 against the index’s −19.91% — still 12.3 points, like for like.

09

How it was rebuilt

  1. Point-in-time universe

    The paper says “S&P 500” but never says which 500. Wikipedia's index change log was parsed and rewound from today's membership to 1 January 2015, recovering 503 constituents. Only 370 have a complete 2014–2022 price history in the local mirror.

  2. Alpha158 features

    All 158 Qlib Alpha158 features (9 k-bar, 4 price, 29 rolling × 5 windows) reimplemented from the handler spec over a 2414 × 370 panel. 17 representative features across every family were checked against an independent pandas implementation and match to float32 precision.

  3. Forward-return forecaster

    Encoder-only Transformer, 10-day lookback, linear head, trained on 2015–2019 and selected on 2020 validation IC — the HLC's signal source. A ridge on the same panel was fitted as a control to separate signal quality from architecture.

  4. Trading environment

    FinRL-style multi-stock env after Liu et al. (2018): state [p, h, b], integer share lots capped at hmax = 100, $1M initial capital, 0.1% proportional cost, execution at the open. Cash-sequenced fills in randomised order so no name is starved by its index position.

  5. Bi-level controllers

    PPO high-level controller over a factorised 3N direction space, DDPG low-level controller sizing each trade. Both written once and shared with the standalone baselines, so the measured gap is the hierarchy and not two different libraries.

  6. Phased alternating training

    Each iteration collects a rollout, blends the HLC reward as αt·alignment + (1−αt)·LLC return with αt = e−0.001t, then updates both controllers. 5×105 steps, checkpoint selected on 2020 Sharpe, evaluated once on 2021 and once on 2022.

10

Data still needed

News text and sentiment scoresdead end
The full HRT scores each stock daily from 10 sampled news items through fingpt-sentiment_llama2-13b_lora. Everything reported here is therefore HRT-FR, the paper's own no-sentiment ablation. This has now been probed with a live token rather than assumed: /news/{symbol} caps at 200 items, ignores every date parameter (from/to, start/end, published_after) and ignores paging, serving only the trailing fortnight; since/until returns HTTP 500. The 2015–2019 training window is unreachable, so this channel cannot be built from this source at any scale of hardware. The workable route is the dataset HRT's own v2 revision uses — FNSPID, via the FinRL-DeepSeek benchmark, which ships precomputed LLM sentiment and risk scores — but that moves the universe from the S&P 500 to an 89-name Nasdaq set, making it a different experiment rather than a port.
Prices for delisted securitiesblocking
133 of the 503 point-in-time 2015 constituents have no usable history here, and 108 of those 133 are names the index itself removed between 2015 and 2022 — ACE, ALTR, ALXN, APC, BBBY, BRCM, CA and the rest of the acquired-or-failed cohort. The surviving 370 are not a random sample. CRSP, Sharadar or Norgate would close the gap; yfinance, the paper's stated source, cannot retrieve most of these tickers at all.
Price-only OHLCVclosed
findata's close is split and dividend adjusted while the paper's Yahoo convention is unstated, so a price-only panel was reconstructed from the dividends table by unwinding the cumulative adjustment factor back from the vendor's own 2026 reference bar. It validates to within 0.5% of known raw opens for names without spin-offs (AAPL −0.0%, MSFT −0.0%, XOM −0.1%, JNJ −0.5%, KO −0.2%); IBM and AT&T are off by 4% and 20% because Kyndryl and Warner Bros. Discovery were spin-offs, not cash dividends, and a dividend-only unwind cannot recover them. The measured tailwind is 2.63 points in 2021 and 2.26 in 2022 — enough to matter against a headline 2022 return of +2.29%, but not enough to change any conclusion here.
The authors' universe and label codeunavailable
No implementation was ever released. Point-in-time versus current-list membership, the handling of names that delisted mid-sample, and the exact label expression all have to be inferred from prose. The reproduction pins each choice explicitly and reports both readings where the prose is ambiguous.
